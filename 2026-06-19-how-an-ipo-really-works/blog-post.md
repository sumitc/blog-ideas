# How an IPO Really Works

SpaceX’s IPO last week was a historic moment indeed. Priced at $135 a share, about 4x oversubscribed, and raising a record $75 billion, the stock opened at $150 valuing the company at roughly $1.77 trillion on day one — highest anyone has ever seen!

Moments like that force you to ask a bigger question: how does an IPO work, and how does the money really flow?

**Step 1: Creation**

Every IPO starts with ownership. A company creates shares that represent its value, held by founders, employees, and early investors. Going public means offering some of those shares to outside investors. It is the first real shift from private ownership to public participation, where the company’s story becomes something the market can buy into.

**Step 2: Preparation**

This is where most of the heavy lifting happens. The company works with investment banks to shape the deal, decide the valuation, and set the price range. Then comes the roadshow, where the business is pitched to institutional investors like mutual funds, pension funds, and hedge funds. This phase is about building demand and testing confidence before the issue closes.

**Step 3: Pricing, Closing, and Allotment**

Once the IPO book closes, the bankers finalize the offer price based on demand. Investors have already put in money during the application process, usually through blocked funds. After allotment is finalized, the **fresh capital from the new shares is transferred to the company,** while any existing shares sold by founders or early investors pay out to those sellers separately. **This is the moment the money is truly raised** — not when the stock first starts trading.

**Step 4: Listing and Growth**

Only after allotment does the stock actually list and begin trading on the exchange. That is when retail investors receive their shares in demat form and the market sets the first live price. From there, the company enters the public-market era — where the capital has been raised, but the real scrutiny begins. Investors now judge growth, margins, governance, and execution every quarter.

**The bigger lesson for founders**

An IPO is not just a funding event. It is a credibility event. If you are starting up or running a company, the real lesson is that public markets reward businesses that can handle attention, discipline, and long-term accountability. The best IPOs do not just create liquidity — they create a stronger company.
